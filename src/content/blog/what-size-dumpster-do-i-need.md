# What Size Dumpster Do I Need? A Practical Sizing Guide

"What size dumpster do I need" is the first question for any project, and the honest answer is that it depends on two things: how much debris you have, and how heavy it is. Get the weight part wrong and you pay for it. This practical guide walks through sizing by project type, with the one rule that matters most in Southwest Florida, where so much debris is dense.

## The One Rule That Decides Everything

Before the project-by-project guide, internalize this: a dumpster has a volume limit and a weight limit, and heavy debris hits the weight limit long before it fills the space. So:

- **Light, bulky debris** (furniture, drywall, cardboard, shingles) is limited by volume. You size up.
- **Heavy, dense debris** (concrete, tile, brick, dirt) is limited by weight. You use a small box filled partway.

In Southwest Florida, tile roofs and concrete are everywhere, so this rule decides more sizing here than almost anywhere. If your debris is dense, ignore how big the pile looks and size by weight. For the full explanation, see [dumpster sizes explained](/blog/dumpster-sizes-explained/) and [dumpster weight limits by size](/blog/dumpster-weight-limits-by-size/).

## Sizing by Project

### Bathroom remodel

A single bathroom tear-out is a small job. A 10- or 15-yard box usually covers it. If it includes a lot of tile, that is heavy, so lean toward a 10-yard and expect to fill it partway.

### Kitchen remodel

Cabinets, counters, flooring and fixtures are bulky but mostly moderate weight. A 15- or 20-yard box fits most kitchen remodels. Stone counters and tile floors add weight, so flag those to the hauler.

### Multi-room or whole-home remodel

A 20-yard box is the workhorse for debris from several rooms. A full gut of a whole home steps up to a 30-yard, often with a mid-job swap.

### Roof replacement

A shingle re-roof on a typical single-family home fits a 20-yard box. A tile roof is the big exception, tile weighs three to four times what shingles do, so a tile tear-off goes in a 10-yard box sized by weight. See [dumpsters on paver driveways](/blog/dumpsters-on-paver-driveways/) for protecting your driveway during a roof job, and note some cities, like North Port, set a minimum roof-dumpster size.

### Garage, attic or household cleanout

A 15- or 20-yard box handles a garage-and-attic cleanout of household items. A full estate or whole-house cleanout usually needs a 20-yard, or a 30 for a very full property.

### Concrete, brick, tile or dirt

Always a 10-yard box, filled only partway to the hauler's fill line. These are the densest common materials, and a bigger box just means overage charges on a container that looks nearly empty. See the [concrete and heavy debris page](/concrete-dumpster-rental/).

### New construction or large demolition

A 30-yard box for high-volume, bulky-but-light debris, often with scheduled swaps. A 40-yard only for commercial-scale, high-volume, low-weight loads. Split heavy block, concrete and mortar into a separate small box.

## A Mixed Job? Split the Debris

Most real projects produce both light and heavy debris. A gut job is mostly light drywall and wood, but a tile floor or a masonry feature adds weight that can push a big box over its limit. The fix: keep the heavy material in its own partly-filled 10-yard box, and the light bulky material in a larger box sized by volume. This is almost always cheaper than cramming everything into one oversized container and paying overage.

## When in Doubt, Describe It

The fastest path to the right size is to describe your debris to a local hauler, the material, a rough volume, and whether it is heavy, and let them size it by weight. You can get a starting recommendation from the [dumpster size calculator](/tools/dumpster-size-calculator/), then confirm on the phone. A good hauler would rather right-size the box up front than deal with an overweight pickup later.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What is the most common dumpster size for a home project?**
The 20-yard roll-off is the most common for home projects, because it fits the broad middle: multi-room remodels, shingle re-roofs and full household cleanouts. It holds about six to eight pickup-truck loads with low walls that are easy to load by hand. The main exception is heavy debris like concrete and tile, which hits the weight limit early and goes in a smaller 10-yard box filled partway instead.

**What size dumpster do I need for a kitchen remodel?**
A 15- or 20-yard box fits most kitchen remodels, covering cabinets, counters, flooring and fixtures, which are bulky but mostly moderate weight. If the kitchen has stone countertops or a tile floor, flag that to the hauler, since tile and stone add weight that can affect the sizing. For a kitchen remodel that is part of a larger whole-home gut, step up to a 20- or 30-yard box.

**What size dumpster do I need for a 2,000 square foot roof?**
For a shingle roof, a 20-yard box typically handles a single-family re-roof in that range, sometimes with a mid-job swap for a larger or steeper roof. For a tile roof of the same size, you need a smaller box sized by weight, usually a 10-yard filled partway, because tile weighs three to four times what shingles do. Tell the hauler the roof's square footage and material, and note some cities set a minimum roof-dumpster size.

**Is it better to get one big dumpster or two small ones?**
It depends on the debris. For heavy material, two smaller boxes, or one small box emptied and returned, is often cheaper than one big box, because a big box filled with dense debris hits its weight limit and racks up overage anyway. For light, bulky debris, one appropriately sized box is usually cheaper. For a mixed job, splitting heavy debris into its own small box and light debris into a larger one is the cost-saving approach.
